Between 2018 and 2019, two Boeing 737 Max planes crashed, killing hundreds of people. The most direct cause of the crashes was the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS), which Boeing had implemented to prevent stall, pointing the plane nose down too far. Digging into the crashes, however, reveals that there is far more to the story. From corporate pressures to self-regulation, there were more problems with the development of the Maxes than meets the eye.

My classmates and I were asked to conduct an ethical analysis of the Boeing 737 Max crashes. We considered five main questions, found solutions to the questions, and then identified constraints to implementing those solutions.

1. To what extent should the FAA have investigates and verified claims made by Boeing during the self-regulation process?

During the certification process of the 737 Max, the FAA delegated some regulatory powers to Boeing. In an attempt to cut costs, Boeing told the FAA that the Maxes were similar enough to the normal 737s that pilots should not need extra training for the new planes. Boeing also did not disclose certain flight simulation failures. It is possible that the crashes could have been avoided had Boeing been more transparent with the FAA.

The obvious solution to this problem would be to ban delegation and self-regulation. Unfortunately, that is not practical given staffing shortages at the FAA as well as time and budget constraints.

2. How should Boeing have balanced their competing priorities of safety and profit?

Employees at Boeing reported toxic work conditions that prioritized speed and profit over thorough engineering. Unfortunately, the rush to certify the 737 Max is part of what caused the crashes.

Ultimately, safety is the most important consideration on any engineering project, as indicated in the first ASME canon. With that being said, it is impossible to guarantee 100% safety. Companies must take every step in their power to produce effective products, and there must be outside regulatory bodies to uphold standards.

3. Should Boeing engineers have disobeyed their managers by reporting MCAS to the FAA?

Though engineers pushed back on the 737 Max internally, Boeing higher-ups downplayed or ignored their concerns. There should have been stronger whistleblower protections that would have made engineers more comfortable raising their concerns with the FAA directly.
